# English output example

> This is a fictional formatting example. Its numbers and “sources” are not evidence about a real event.

## Verdict

**Core claim:** “A single policy change in City X cut air pollution by 90% in one month.”<br>
**Truth estimate:** 30% · **Research confidence:** Medium<br>
**Decision:** Rebut the percentage and causal claim; concede that pollution fell.

## Claim audit

| Atomic claim | Estimate | Verdict | Decisive point |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Pollution fell | 80% | Mostly supported | The measurement series shows a decline |
| The decline was 90% | 20% | Unsupported | Comparable measurements show roughly 30% |
| The policy was the sole cause | 25% | Unsupported | Weather and seasonality were not isolated |

## Ready-to-use reply

> “The decline is real, but the comparable measurement series does not show 90%. And attributing the whole change to one policy requires an analysis that separates weather and seasonal effects. The defensible claim is that the policy may have contributed—not that it single-handedly caused a 90% drop.”

## Why it works

1. It does not deny the real decline.
2. It focuses on the two load-bearing errors: magnitude and causality.
3. It states what evidence would settle the dispute.

## Likely comeback → answer

**Comeback:** “But the graph clearly goes down.”<br>
**Answer:** “It does. The dispute is not whether it fell; it is whether the fall was 90% and whether one policy caused all of it. The graph alone establishes neither.”

## Do not claim

- “The policy had no effect.”
- “Every article about this is lying.”
