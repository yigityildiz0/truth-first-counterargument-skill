# Argument engine

Use this after the evidence ledger is stable enough to choose a response route.

## Contents

1. Steelman and map
2. Find the load-bearing point
3. Choose the attack type
4. Construct the response

## 1. Steelman and map

Write the strongest fair version of the target in one sentence. Then map:

```text
Conclusion
├─ stated premise(s)
├─ evidence offered
├─ hidden premise(s)
├─ definition(s)
└─ inference connecting premises to conclusion
```

If the user would reject the steelman even when neutrally phrased, explain the disagreement before drafting a reply.

## 2. Find the load-bearing point

Ask which single component must hold for the conclusion to work. Prefer attacking that component over collecting minor errors.

Typical load-bearing weaknesses:

- a false or unsupported premise;
- a misleading denominator or base rate;
- an invalid causal step;
- an equivocal definition;
- an unrepresentative example;
- a hidden universal claim inside vague wording;
- an unspoken value priority;
- evidence that proves a narrower claim than the speaker asserts;
- a source chain that ends in one unverified origin.

Do not lead with spelling errors, personality judgments, or irrelevant hypocrisy.

## 3. Choose the attack type

### Factual correction

Use when a material statement conflicts with strong evidence. State the correction, scope, and source.

### Scope correction

Use when “some” becomes “all,” one period becomes permanent, one country becomes universal, or a subgroup becomes a population.

### Causal correction

Use when timing, correlation, selection, reverse causality, confounding, or a missing mechanism breaks the inference.

### Definition challenge

Use when the conclusion changes depending on terms such as freedom, democracy, communism, liberalism, success, violence, poverty, or consensus. Offer an operational definition rather than arguing over labels.

### Burden and evidence challenge

Use when a confident claim is unsupported. Ask for the evidence that would distinguish it from competing explanations. Do not treat lack of proof as proof of the opposite.

### Internal critique

Accept the target's own stated values or premises temporarily, then show that the conclusion does not follow or creates a contradiction.

### Trade-off critique

Use when the dispute is normative. Acknowledge the intended benefit and identify the neglected cost, right, incentive, distributional effect, or second-order consequence.

### Counterexample

Use only when it logically defeats a universal claim. One example rarely disproves a statistical tendency.

### Decisive question

Use when one missing definition, denominator, comparison, or causal mechanism determines the dispute. The question must be answerable and relevant, not a trap.

## 4. Construct the response

Use this sequence when possible:

1. **Concede what is true.** This removes easy defensive replies.
2. **Name the exact disagreement.** Avoid “everything here is wrong.”
3. **Give the decisive evidence or inference.** One or two points are usually enough.
4. **Connect it back to the conclusion.** Explain why the target no longer follows.
5. **End with a clear question or corrected formulation.**

Compact formula:

> “X kısmı doğru; fakat buradan Y sonucu çıkmıyor. Çünkü [decisive reason]. En doğru ifade [narrow corrected claim].”

English equivalent:

> “X is fair, but it does not establish Y. The decisive issue is [reason]. The evidence supports the narrower claim [correction].”

## 5. Stress-test likely comebacks

Generate the strongest plausible reply from an informed opponent—not a weak caricature. Test:

- Did the response overstate certainty?
- Can the opponent change the definition without being noticed?
- Is the source independent and directly relevant?
- Does the evidence show causation or only association?
- Was a concession omitted that would neutralize the objection?
- Is the response attacking a claim the speaker did not make?

Revise until the strongest comeback either fails on evidence or reveals a genuine unresolved value disagreement.

Limit the comeback ladder to two levels by default. Do not script endless escalation.

## 6. Control tone and length

Offer the minimum number of variants needed:

- **Everyday / halk ağzı**: natural, short, non-technical;
- **Calm and precise**: default for public comments;
- **Detailed**: sources and reasoning for a longer exchange;
- **Question-led**: useful when the opponent's missing premise is decisive.

Match force to evidence. Strong language requires strong, direct evidence. Never increase hostility because the user describes the target dismissively.

## 7. Avoid common failures

- **Fallacy-name dumping**: Explain the broken inference; labels alone persuade nobody.
- **Gish gallop**: Do not answer ten weak points with twenty weak points. Group and address the load-bearing claims.
- **Source prestige substitution**: “A famous outlet said it” is not the argument. Cite the underlying evidence.
- **Quote mining**: Include the qualifier that materially changes meaning.
- **Motte-and-bailey**: Freeze the original claim before it is softened or expanded.
- **False balance**: Do not give equal weight to evidence merely because two sides exist.
- **Reverse dogmatism**: Refuting one argument does not prove the opposite ideology or worldview.
- **Mind reading**: Do not infer hidden motives without evidence.
- **Personal victory framing**: Replace “make them unable to answer” with “make the claim withstand informed scrutiny.”
